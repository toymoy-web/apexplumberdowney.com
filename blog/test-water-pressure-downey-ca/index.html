How to Test Water Pressure at Home in Downey, CA

A one-minute test, a table to read the result, and what to do if it is too low or too high.

By the Downey Apex Plumbing team · Updated October 12, 2026

To test your home's water pressure, screw a pressure gauge onto an outdoor hose bib, turn the faucet on with no other water running, and read the dial. A reading of about 50 to 70 psi is comfortable for most homes. Anything above 80 psi calls for a regulator under the California Plumbing Code, and anything below about 40 psi usually feels weak in the shower.

The test takes a minute and costs less than a meal. It tells you whether your pipes, your fixtures, and your water heater are living under stress.

If It Is Low

Common causes of weak pressure

Check these in order

  • 1A partly closed main shutoff or meter-side valve.
  • 2Clogged aerators on faucets and showerheads, a common issue with hard water.
  • 3A failing pressure regulator.
  • 4Corroded or narrowed supply pipe.
  • 5A leak on the service line between the meter and the house.

Start with the cheap items. Unscrew an aerator and look for white scale. If one fixture is weak and the others are fine, it is that fixture.

If every fixture is weak, look at the main valve and the regulator. If the meter spins with everything off, a leak may be stealing pressure, and our leak finding service finds it.

If It Is High

Why too much pressure is a problem

High pressure strains every joint and every appliance. It shortens the life of water heaters, washing machine hoses, and toilet fill valves. It also makes a small leak lose far more water. The usual fixes are a new regulator and, in a closed system, a thermal expansion tank on the water heater.

Signs of high pressure include banging pipes, a toilet that hisses after it fills, and a relief valve that drips. A reading above 80 psi confirms it. If the regulator is more than ten to fifteen years old, replacement is reasonable.

Related Fixes

Hammer arrestors and expansion tanks

PartWhat it doesWhen it is needed
Pressure regulatorReduces incoming pressureWhen pressure exceeds about 80 psi
Thermal expansion tankAbsorbs pressure rise as water heatsWhen the system is closed, with a regulator or check valve
Water hammer arrestorCushions sudden shutoffsWhen pipes bang at fixtures or appliances
Isolation valvesLet you work on one sectionAny renovation

A closed system traps the extra volume of water as it heats, and the pressure rises. That is why a regulator often goes together with an expansion tank on the water heater. A tank with a relief valve that drips regularly is a hint. A plumber can check the air charge in the expansion tank with a gauge in a minute.

Replacing a regulator is usually a short job, but it should be done with the water off and the line relieved. Ask the plumber to set the pressure and show you the reading at the hose bib afterward.

What to tell a plumber about pressure

  • 1Your reading at the hose bib, and the time of day.
  • 2Whether it changes between morning and evening.
  • 3Whether you hear banging or hissing.
  • 4Whether the water heater relief valve has dripped.
  • 5The age of the regulator, if you know it.

Pressure that is right for the system is quiet. Fixtures last longer, the water heater lasts longer, and a small leak stays small. It is among the cheapest changes you can make to a house.

Worked Scenarios

Three readings and what we would do

The same house can give different readings at different times of day. Utility pressure often dips in the evening when many people use water, and rises overnight. A gauge with a drag needle records the highest reading while you are away, which is a good way to catch overnight spikes.

If you replace a regulator, set it to the middle of the comfortable range, around 60 psi, and re-check at the hose bib. A reading you can write on a sticky note next to the valve is a gift to the next person who works on the house.

In older homes with narrowed pipe, a high reading at the hose bib can still come with weak flow at a distant shower. The gauge measures pressure with no flow, and weak flow under demand points to restriction in the pipe, not the regulator.

If you rent and suspect a pressure problem, report it in writing with your gauge reading and the date. A reading is a fact, and it moves the conversation from "my water feels weird" to "the pressure at the hose bib is 96 psi." A landlord or a plumber can act on the second statement. Owners can use the same reading to decide whether a regulator belongs on this year's repair list.

The 80 psi limit comes from the California Plumbing Code, which is built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC). The code treats pressure above that figure as a reason to install a regulator, so a reading over 80 psi is not a matter of taste. It is a point at which the code expects a fix.
