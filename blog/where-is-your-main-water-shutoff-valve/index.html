Where Is Your Main Water Shutoff Valve?

How to find it, test it, and use it in a hurry, written for Downey homes and the cities around them.

By the Downey Apex Plumbing team · Updated October 3, 2026

Your main water shutoff valve is usually on the front wall of the house, near the hose bib, or in the garage where the supply line comes in. It is a round wheel or a lever on the pipe, and you turn a wheel clockwise or a lever a quarter turn to stop all water into the house. Finding it before an emergency is one of the cheapest bits of preparation a homeowner can do.

When a pipe splits, the minutes before the water is off decide how much of the house gets wet. People who know where the valve is shut off the flow in seconds. People who do not spend those minutes searching.

Where to Look

The usual places for the valve

PlaceWhat to look forNotes
Front of the houseA pipe rising from the ground near the hose bibCommon on slab homes
Garage wallA pipe entering near the water heaterOften a wheel handle
Side of the houseA pipe coming out of the wall or groundCheck behind shrubs
Utility closet or basement areaNear the water heater or laundryLess common here
By the curb, in a boxThe utility's meter and valveNot yours to operate

If you cannot find it, trace the line. Follow the water pipe from the hose bib or the water heater back toward the wall where it enters the house. The main valve sits on that line before it branches. The meter box near the curb is a separate item, and the valve inside it belongs to the utility.

Valve Types

Gate valve or ball valve

TypeHandleHow it fails
Gate valveRound wheel you turn several timesCan seize or leak at the stem, and the gate can break loose
Ball valveLever that turns a quarter turnMore reliable and quick to operate

Many older homes have gate valves. They work for years and then stop working the day you need them. Replacing a gate valve with a ball valve is a small job that pays for itself the first time you need to shut the water off in a hurry. Our burst line repair page explains the first minutes of an emergency.

Shutting It Off

A four-step routine you can practice

1

Find it

Locate the valve and clear anything in front of it.

2

Close it

Wheel clockwise until it stops, or lever a quarter turn.

3

Check it

Open a faucet. Water should slow to a trickle and stop.

4

Reopen it

Turn it back on slowly, then open a faucet to bleed air.

What to do if you cannot close the valve

  • 1Do not use tools to force a seized valve. It can snap.
  • 2Look for a second shutoff at the fixture that is leaking, such as the valve under a sink.
  • 3Turn off the water heater if the leak is on the hot side.
  • 4Call for help and describe what you see.
  • 5If water is rising fast, move belongings and photograph the damage.
Fixture Valves

The small ones under sinks and behind toilets

Every sink and toilet should have its own shutoff, usually a small oval or round handle on a supply line. The California Plumbing Code, built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), expects an accessible shutoff at each fixture. These valves let you stop one leak without losing water to the whole house.

They also seize when they are never turned. Try each one gently once a year. If a handle will not move, or if it weeps water when turned, replace the valve before you need it, not during a leak.

In Downey

What local conditions mean for your valve

The City of Downey Water Division targets about 65 psi at its water yard, and pressure at a house can be higher. At that pressure, a split supply line moves a lot of water quickly, which is why the shutoff matters. Its Utilities Division also reads about 22,500 meters every two months, so a leak that runs between readings can add up before anyone notices.

Census-based counts show roughly 13,400 Downey homes were built in the 1950s. Original shutoff valves in homes that old are often gate valves that have not been turned in decades. If you own one of those homes, testing the valve this weekend is time well spent.

If you are in a rental, tell your landlord where the valve is, or ask them. Our South Gate page covers renters and older homes in more detail.

Common Mistakes

Why people cannot shut the water off in time

MistakeWhat happensBetter habit
Storing boxes in front of the valveYou lose precious seconds clearing a pathKeep a clear 3-foot area
Never testing the valveIt seizes, and the handle spins or snapsTurn it once a year
Closing the utility's valve at the meterYou may damage it, and it is not yours to operateUse your own valve
Painting over the handlePaint glues the stem and hides the handleScrape and label it
Not telling the householdOnly one person knows where it isShow everyone, including older children

A tag on the valve helps. A simple paper or plastic tag that says MAIN WATER, with an arrow showing which way to close, takes thirty seconds to make and removes guesswork at the worst moment. Take a photo of the valve and keep it in your phone, in case you are away when someone else needs it.

If the valve will not close fully or drips at the stem, replace it before an emergency, and have the line behind it checked, since water line replacement covers the pipe that feeds it. Teach the routine to everyone who lives in the house. A teenager who knows how to close the valve can save a house when you are not home. A tenant who knows where it is can limit damage before the landlord arrives.

A short vacation checklist

  • 1Close the main shutoff before a long trip.
  • 2Turn the water heater to its vacation setting or off, following the label.
  • 3Open a faucet at the lowest point to drain pressure, then close it.
  • 4Ask a neighbor to check the house if you are gone for weeks.
  • 5Restore water slowly when you return and check under sinks.

None of this costs much. The valve is already in your house. The only investment is knowing where it is and keeping it working, and that is the difference between a mop-up and a remodel when something fails.
